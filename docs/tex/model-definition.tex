\section{Motivation}

The standard empirical treatment of \snia\ light curves, exemplified by \salt\
\citep{Guy2007}, models the spectral energy distribution as a linear expansion about a
mean sequence,
\begin{equation}
  F(p,\lambda) = x_{0}\left[M_{0}(p,\lambda) + x_{1}M_{1}(p,\lambda) + \dots\right]
                 \exp\!\left[\colr\,\mathrm{CL}(\lambda)\right],
\end{equation}
with per-supernova parameters $(x_{0}, x_{1}, \colr, t_{0})$: a description of a
\emph{surface} over phase and wavelength, from which the light curve in any band
follows by integration against a filter response. The parameter $x_{1}$ is read as a
``stretch'', but it does not act as a reparameterization of time --- it sets the
amplitude of a fixed additive component --- so shape and timing are entangled in one
number.

The geometric alternative starts elsewhere. A supernova observed in $n$ bands
supplies a point in $\Rn$ at each epoch, and over the event those points trace a
curve. The curve is independent of when it was sampled, and by the fundamental
theorem of curves it is determined, up to a rigid motion, by $n-1$ scalar invariants
of arclength. Shape then lives entirely in those invariants and timing entirely in
the arclength map, since a reparameterization of time changes how fast a supernova
moves along its curve but does not change the curve.

That separation is exact as a statement about curves, and is not thereby a guarantee about
fits: over a finite, noisy window a change in the traversal and a change in curvature can
mimic one another. Unlike the exact redundancies of Section~\ref{sec:pars}, which
are removed by convention, how far the two can be told apart is an empirical question.


We work in magnitude space, with the Euclidean metric
$\mathrm{diag}(1,1)$. Both are choices, argued at length in
\texttt{docs/model-note-backlog.md}: the space because distance and dust (to first order)
act there as translations, to which curvature is blind; the metric because none is
physically preferred, so one must be declared. The likelihood, by contrast, is evaluated
in native flux (Section~\ref{sec:samples}).

The primary configuration is \ztf\ $g$ and $r$ \citep{Bellm2019}, where $n=2$ and
$\curve = (\Magg, \Magr)$, so focus on $n=2$.

\section{The model}

\subsection{Light curves as traversal over curves in magnitude space}
\label{sec:curve}
The rest-frame multi-band light-curve template, as a function of phase $\phase$ measured
from $g$ maximum, is denoted as
\begin{equation}
  \curve(\phase)
    = \bigl(\Magg(\phase), \Magr(\phase)\bigr) \in \Rtwo.
  \label{eq:curvedef}
\end{equation}
$\curve$ is a parameterized curve in the $2$-dimensional magnitude space.
The relation between phase and arclength along the curve is $\arc(\phase)$, defined by
$\dd \arc = \norm{\dd\curve/\dd \phase}\,\dd \phase$ with the constant of integration
fixed by $\arc = 0$ at $g$ maximum. Wherever $\norm{\dd\curve/\dd\phase} \neq 0$ this is
strictly increasing and invertible, and the curve reparameterized as $\curve(\arc)$ is
unit-speed, $\norm{\curve'(\arc)} = 1$.\footnote{Since
$\norm{\dd\curve/\dd\phase} = \norm{\curve'(\arc)}\,\abs{\dd\arc/\dd\phase}
= \abs{\dd\arc/\dd\phase}$, every band is stationary together exactly where
$\dd\arc/\dd\phase = 0$. That is the monotonicity requirement \eqref{eq:monotone} imposed
in Section~\ref{sec:pars}, so the condition is not an extra assumption; it is unrelated to
the turning condition \eqref{eq:turncond}.}
The template is carried on a fixed arclength interval $\arc \in [\arc_{\min}, \arclen]$
with $\arc_{\min} < 0 < \arclen$, declared once for the model rather than fitted and wide
enough to cover the modelled phase range for every supernova. Every global function below
is a function on this interval, and it is $\arclen$ that appears in the gauge conditions
of Section~\ref{sec:pars}. What varies between supernovae is which phases these arclengths
map to, not the interval itself.
A unit-speed curve in $\Rtwo$ admits a Frenet frame,
 \begin{equation}
  \curve' = \tang, \qquad
  \tang' = \curv\,\norml.
  \label{eq:frenet2d}
\end{equation}
with $\tang$ the unit \emph{tangent} and $\norml$ the \emph{normal}, the counterclockwise
$90^{\circ}$ rotation of $\tang$; the \emph{curvature} $\curv$ is correspondingly signed.


We now map out the inverse: how a curve, specified by curvature and three initial conditions,
 and the traversal over that curve, specified by a function,
define light curves.


Given $\curv$, $\curve(\arc)$ is determined through the above differential equations and
initial conditions for the direction of $\tang$ and the position of $\curve$. Writing the
unit tangent by its angle $\psi$, the equations are solved with
\begin{equation}
  \tang(\arc) = \bigl(\cos\psi(\arc),\ \sin\psi(\arc)\bigr),
  \qquad
  \norml(\arc) = \bigl(-\sin\psi(\arc),\ \cos\psi(\arc)\bigr),
  \label{eq:tangangle}
\end{equation}
and $\psi' = \curv$. Curvature is thus the rate of turning of the tangent, and two
quadratures give $\psi$ and the curve,
\begin{align}
  \psi(\arc)   &= \psi_{0} + \int_{{0}}^{\arc}\curv(\zeta)\,\dd\zeta ,
                  \label{eq:turning}\\
  \curve(\arc) &= \curve_{0} + \int_{{0}}^{\arc}
                  \bigl(\cos\psi(\zeta),\ \sin\psi(\zeta)\bigr)\,\dd\zeta .
                  \label{eq:quadrature}
\end{align}
We choose $\curve_{0} = (0,0)$ and $\psi_{0} = -\pi/2$, so that $\Magg'(0) = 0$ with
$\Magr'(0) < 0$: $g$ is at an extremum while $r$ is still brightening, hence $r$ peaks
after $g$, as observed. The extremum is peak brightness, a minimum of $\Magg$, only if
\begin{equation}
  \curv(0) > 0 ,
  \label{eq:peakcond}
\end{equation}
since $\Magg'' = -\sin\psi\,\curv$; and it is the only one on the interval only if
\begin{equation}
  0 < \abs{\Psi(\arc)} < \pi \qquad (\arc \neq 0) ,
  \label{eq:turncond}
\end{equation}
where $\Psi(\arc) = \int_{0}^{\arc}\curv$ is the turning since maximum and
$\Magg' = \cos\psi$ vanishes exactly at $\Psi \in \pi\mathbb{Z}$. Only \eqref{eq:peakcond}
is imposed in Section~\ref{sec:pars}; where \eqref{eq:turncond} fails, $\peakmag$ and
$\colr$ are not the peak magnitude and color.
Setting the template $g$ magnitude and color to zero at maximum makes $\peakmag$ the peak
$g$ magnitude and $\colr$ the color at peak, following \salt's practice of
normalizing at maximum --- there in $B$, here in $g$.

The connection to supernova phase is specified by the inverse of $s(p)$,  the function $p(s)$.

\subsection{Per-supernova light-curve model}
\label{sec:pars}

We seek to construct a light curve model that captures the diversity of SN~Ia
behavior.
Parameters considered in traditional models include
\begin{itemize}
\item $\peakmag$: The apparent peak $g$ magnitude, contributing a term
  $\peakmag\,\mathbf{1}$ added to $\curve$. It is achromatic, and absorbs the absolute
  peak magnitude, the distance modulus and any grey offset together; separating them is
  the downstream standardization, so the distance modulus $\dm$ is never fitted here.
\item $\colr$: A phase-independent shift in color.  This contributes a term
  $ \colr\,\dustunit$ added to $\curve$, with $\dustunit = (0,-1)$, so that $\colr$ is the
  shift in $\Magg - \Magr$. Since $\mathbf{1}$ and $\dustunit$ span $\Rtwo$, the placement
  $\peakmag\,\mathbf{1} + \colr\,\dustunit$ is an arbitrary translation and this basis is
  declared, not measured: a different choice would only change what $\colr$ counts.
\item $\xstretch$: A wavelength-independent dilation of the light curve, corresponding
  to `stretch': a supernova with $\xstretch > 1$ evolves more slowly, reaching each point
  of the template at $\xstretch$ times the mean phase.
  \end{itemize}

The motivation for describing light-curve shapes this way is apparent: in this
parameterization the curvature is unchanged. The terms $\peakmag\,\mathbf{1}$ and
$\colr\,\dustunit$ translate the curve, and $\xstretch$ only alters the rate of travel
along it.

To go beyond traditional light-curve models, we explore two new per-supernova parameters,
\begin{itemize}
\item $x_p$: Variation in time evolution beyond that described by $s$, with
\begin{equation}
  \phase(\arc; \xstretch, x_p) = \xstretch\,p_{0}(\arc) + x_p\,p_{1}(\arc),
  \label{eq:travlin}
\end{equation}
where $p_0$ and $p_1$ are functions obeying $p_{0}(0) = p_{1}(0) = 0$, so that $\phase = 0$
at $g$ maximum for every supernova. The traversal must increase,
\begin{equation}
  \deriv{\phase}{\arc} = \xstretch\,p_{0}'(\arc) + x_p\,p_{1}'(\arc) > 0
  \qquad \text{on } [\arc_{\min}, \arclen],
  \label{eq:monotone}
\end{equation}
a joint condition on the coefficients and the basis rather than positivity of $\xstretch$
alone. Taking $p_{0}' > 0$ and writing $R = p_{1}'/p_{0}'$, the left side is affine in $R$,
so \eqref{eq:monotone} holds throughout iff it holds at the extremes of $R$:
\begin{equation}
  \xstretch + x_p R_{\min} > 0, \qquad \xstretch + x_p R_{\max} > 0 ,
  \label{eq:wedge}
\end{equation}
the extremes being attained since $R$ is continuous on a compact interval with
$p_{0}' > 0$. This is a wedge in $(\xstretch, x_p)$ with apex at the origin. It implies
$\xstretch > 0$ exactly when $0 \in [R_{\min}, R_{\max}]$, that is when $p_{1}'$ changes
sign, $\xstretch$ being then a convex combination of the two left-hand sides; otherwise
$\xstretch < 0$ is admissible. The traversal is
linear in $(\xstretch, x_p)$, which is what the fitting uses. With two functions in play
$\xstretch$ no longer corresponds exactly to the `stretch' of \citet{Perlmutter1997}: it
recovers that classical dilation only when $x_p = 0$, and otherwise no single factor
describes the traversal.

There are degenerate solutions to Eq.~\eqref{eq:travlin}. For example,
$p_{0} \rightarrow \alpha\,p_{0} + \beta\,p_{1}$ together with
$\xstretch \rightarrow \xstretch/\alpha$ and
$x_p \rightarrow x_p - (\beta/\alpha)\,\xstretch$ leaves $\phase(\arc; \xstretch, x_p)$
unchanged for every supernova, for any $\alpha > 0$ and $\beta$; the same holds for the
corresponding replacement of $p_{1}$. 
We break this degeneracy by normalizing the population,
\begin{equation}
  \langle \xstretch \rangle = 1, \qquad \langle x_p \rangle = 0, \qquad
  \mathrm{Var}(x_p) = 1, \qquad \mathrm{Cov}(\xstretch, x_p) = 0,
  \label{eq:travgauge}
\end{equation}
one for each parameter of the change of basis: $\langle \xstretch \rangle = 1$ and
$\mathrm{Var}(x_p) = 1$ set the scales of $p_{0}$ and $p_{1}$, $\langle x_p \rangle = 0$
forbids folding $p_{1}$ into $p_{0}$, and $\mathrm{Cov}(\xstretch, x_p) = 0$ forbids
folding $p_{0}$ into $p_{1}$. All four are blind to the sign of $p_{1}$, being unchanged
by $(p_{1}, x_p) \rightarrow (-p_{1}, -x_p)$, so a discrete ambiguity remains; we fix it
by requiring $p_{1}(\arclen) > 0$, with $\arclen$ the arclength at the end of the modelled
range, so that $x_p > 0$ lengthens the traversal. Anchored by $p_{1}(0) = 0$, this is the
accumulated deviation over the whole arc rather than a local value.

\item $\shapekappa$: First-order deviations in curvature, with
\begin{equation}
\curv(\arc; \shapekappa) = \curv_{0}(\arc) + \shapekappa\,\curv_{1}(\arc),
\end{equation}
 where
$\curv_{0}(\arc)$ and $\curv_{1}(\arc)$ are functions. By \eqref{eq:peakcond} the
coefficient is bounded below, $\curv_{0}(0) + \shapekappa\,\curv_{1}(0) > 0$, a constraint
on $\shapekappa$ of the same kind as \eqref{eq:wedge} is on $(\xstretch, x_p)$.

The expansion is linear in $\curv$ but not in magnitudes. Writing
$K_{1}(\arc) = \int_{0}^{\arc}\curv_{1}$, the turning is linear, $\psi = \psi_{0} +
\int_{0}^{\arc}\curv_{0} + \shapekappa K_{1}$, but the curve reaches it through
\eqref{eq:quadrature}, and to first order is displaced by
\begin{equation}
  \delta\curve(\arc) = \shapekappa \int_{0}^{\arc} K_{1}(\zeta)\,\norml(\zeta)\,\dd\zeta ,
  \label{eq:kappadisp}
\end{equation}
a double integral of $\curv_{1}$ carried along the normal. The displacement therefore
accumulates with arclength: a value of $\shapekappa$ negligible near maximum need not be
negligible at the end of the window, and the linearization holds only while
$\abs{\shapekappa K_{1}} \ll 1$. A prior on $\shapekappa$ is a prior on turning, not on
displacement in magnitude space.

The degeneracy in the choice of basis is broken by
\begin{equation}
  \langle \shapekappa \rangle = 0, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(\shapekappa) = 1,
  \qquad \int_{0}^{\arclen}\curv_{1}(\arc)\,\dd\arc > 0 ,
  \label{eq:curvgauge}
\end{equation}
the first making $\curv_{0}$ the mean curvature and the second fixing the scale of
$\curv_{1}$. As in \eqref{eq:travgauge} these leave a sign free, removed by the third:
by \eqref{eq:turning} the integral is the net extra turning contributed by $\curv_{1}$,
so $\shapekappa > 0$ means the track turns further. Both bases are thus fixed by
conditions internal to the functions themselves, never by a correlation between a shape
and a timing parameter, which would be both degenerate for an uncorrelated population and
at odds with the separation the model asserts.
\end{itemize}

The model for a supernova light curve, parameterized
by $\allpars = (\peakmag,\, \colr,\, \xstretch,\, x_p,\, \shapekappa,\, \tmax)$, is then
\begin{align}
  \begin{pmatrix} \magg \\ \magr \end{pmatrix}\!(\arc \mid \allpars)
    &= \curve\bigl(\arc;\, \shapekappa\bigr) \;+\; \peakmag\,\mathbf{1} \;+\; \colr\,\dustunit,
    \label{eq:modelmag}\\
  t(\arc \mid \allpars)
    &= \tmax + (1+\zred)\,\bigl[\xstretch\,p_{0}(\arc) + x_p\,p_{1}(\arc)\bigr],
    \label{eq:modeltime}
\end{align}
where $\curve(\arc; \shapekappa)$ is the curve generated from $\curv(\arc; \shapekappa)$ by
the quadratures \eqref{eq:turning}--\eqref{eq:quadrature}. Equations \eqref{eq:modelmag} and \eqref{eq:modeltime} are the light curve in
parametric form: $\arc$ sweeps the template, \eqref{eq:modeltime} places each of its points
in observer time and \eqref{eq:modelmag} gives the magnitudes there. The
reparameterization $\arc(\phase)$ therefore never has to be constructed in closed form;
evaluating the model at the observed epochs requires only a monotone interpolation of
\eqref{eq:modeltime}, which \eqref{eq:wedge} guarantees. The redshift $\zred$ is known rather than fitted, and
$\phase$ is rest-frame throughout.

\subsection{Modelling functions}
\label{sec:traversal}

The curvature basis $\curv_{0}, \curv_{1}$ and the traversal basis $p_{0}, p_{1}$ are
global functions on $[\arc_{\min}, \arclen]$, represented by neural networks and fit
jointly with the per-supernova parameters. Each carries an explicit amplitude, for instance
$p_{1} = a_{1}\,\NN_{p_{1}}(\arc)$, so that the gauge conditions act on a few scalars
rather than on network weights.

The traversal networks emit \emph{derivatives}, integrated on the arclength grid,
\begin{equation}
  p_{0}'(\arc) = \mathrm{softplus}\,\NN_{p_{0}}(\arc), \qquad
  p_{1}'(\arc) = a_{1}\,\NN_{p_{1}}(\arc), \qquad
  p_{k}(\arc) = \int_{0}^{\arc} p_{k}'(\zeta)\,\dd\zeta .
  \label{eq:travnet}
\end{equation}
The anchors $p_{0}(0) = p_{1}(0) = 0$ and the positivity of $p_{0}'$ assumed in
\eqref{eq:wedge} then hold identically rather than as conditions to be imposed. Applying
the same device to $\dd\phase/\dd\arc$ itself would guarantee monotonicity outright, but at
the cost of the linearity in $(\xstretch, x_p)$ on which \eqref{eq:travlin} rests, so the
wedge is kept as a constraint instead.

What remains divides in two, and the division is what makes it tractable. The gauge
conditions \eqref{eq:travgauge} and \eqref{eq:curvgauge} are moments of the fitted
coefficients and cannot hold a priori, but each is an \emph{exact symmetry} of the
likelihood: imposing one changes no predicted light curve. They are therefore fixed by
projection after each optimization step, never by a penalty, which would trade against the
fit for nothing. For the traversal the projection is closed-form --- writing
$m = \langle u \rangle$ and $S = \mathrm{Cov}(u)$ for $u = (\xstretch, x_p)$, the matrix
$A \in \mathrm{GL}(2,\Real)$ carrying $u \mapsto Au$ into gauge satisfies $(Am)_{1} = 1$,
$(Am)_{2} = 0$, $(ASA^{\top})_{22} = 1$ and $(ASA^{\top})_{12} = 0$, four equations in four
unknowns, while the basis transforms as $P \mapsto A^{-\top}P$ for
$P = (p_{0}, p_{1})^{\top}$, the transpose being what leaves $\phase = u^{\top}P$
invariant. Its second row is
$t\,(-m_{2}, m_{1})$ with $t$ set by the variance condition and its sign by
$p_{1}(\arclen) > 0$; the first row then solves a $2 \times 2$ system. The curvature case
is a shift and a scale. Gauge fixing is a choice of representative, not something the fit
must trade against.

The wedge \eqref{eq:wedge} and the peak condition \eqref{eq:peakcond} are not symmetries:
they restrict the model, and projecting onto them changes the likelihood, as projected
gradient descent should. Each is a small set of linear inequalities on one supernova's
coefficients --- two half-planes for $(\xstretch, x_p)$, one half-line for $\shapekappa$
--- so the projection is closed-form and costs nothing.

The two projections do not interfere.

\begin{proposition}
Gauge fixing cannot move a supernova into or out of the region admitted by
\eqref{eq:wedge} and \eqref{eq:peakcond}, and projection onto that region cannot break the
gauge conditions.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Both constraints are conditions on $\dd\phase/\dd\arc$ and on $\curv$, and both quantities
are unchanged by a change of basis, since $\phase$ and $\curv$ are what the bases
represent. The admissible region is therefore gauge-invariant, and projection onto it acts
only within a gauge orbit.
\end{proof}

\noindent
So the two are applied in either order, once per step, with no iteration between them.
Whether either constraint is \emph{active} is worth recording: the wedge binds when a
supernova's two traversal functions are near collinear, and \eqref{eq:peakcond} binds when
the shape mode would invert the curvature at maximum. Either happening for more than a
few objects is a statement about the parameterization, not a numerical nuisance.

The model as it stands carries no intrinsic scatter: every departure of a supernova from
the six-parameter family must be absorbed by photometric noise. This is a limitation
rather than a claim, and is the first thing to revisit once the six parameters are shown
to earn their cost.


\section{Sample}
\label{sec:samples}

Coverage was measured directly from the \ztf\ SN~Ia DR2 archive
\citep{Rigault2025}. Starting from the 3628 cataloged objects, we require
$\zred < 0.05$ and both the \texttt{lccoverage} and \texttt{fitquality} flags set,
leaving 652 supernovae. Fourteen of these have no light-curve file in the release; they
are exactly the fourteen whose \salt\ $t_{0}$ is NaN, so the absence is of photometry
rather than of shape, and 638 objects remain with light curves. Epochs are counted per
band inside the rest-frame window $[-15,+40]$~d with signal-to-noise above five and with
none of the quality bits excluded by \texttt{flag \& 31 == 0}, the archive default. The
signal-to-noise requirement defines the sample and nothing more: every epoch in the window
enters the likelihood, non-detections and negative fluxes included. The likelihood is
Gaussian in native flux, so a low-signal epoch weights itself down correctly, whereas
discarding one would select on the noise realization of the very quantity being fitted.

The analysis uses the 598 supernovae with at least five good epochs in \emph{each} of $g$
and $r$; the count is 635 at three or more and 464 at ten or more. The $i$ band is not
used: only 178 objects reach five good $i$ epochs, too few to support a third dimension.
Selection and counts are produced by \texttt{dgsn.data.sample}.

\paragraph{A caveat on the window used for counting.} Counting epochs inside
$[-15,+40]$~d requires a date of maximum, but $\tmax$ is precisely what the model fits
(Section~\ref{sec:pars}); the numbers above were obtained using the archive's
\salt\ $t_{0}$ to place the window. This is \salt\ entering upstream of
\emph{selection}, far weaker than \salt\ entering the fit --- no photometry is
transformed, initialized, or K-corrected by it, and the fitted $\tmax$ remains free and
independent. It is nonetheless an exception to the rule that \salt\ stays strictly
downstream, and is recorded here rather than left implicit. The check that settles it is
to recount using an SED-free anchor, for instance a window placed on the brightest good
$g$ epoch, and confirm that the two samples agree in \emph{membership}: equal counts can
hide different objects, so the symmetric difference is the quantity to report. The
fourteen selected objects with NaN $t_{0}$ need no such estimator, having no photometry
to place a window on.

\paragraph{Milky Way extinction.} Galactic reddening is tabulated per object as
\texttt{mwebv}, so its \emph{amplitude} is known and is removed deterministically
before fitting rather than being fitted. What remains free is $\colr$, which absorbs
host reddening and intrinsic color together and does not separate them. Note that what is
known is $E(B-V)$, not $A_{g}(\phase)$ and $A_{r}(\phase)$; the conversion is
phase-dependent and needs an SED, so removing a constant offset is an approximation
here as well. It is not uniformly mild: the sample has median \texttt{mwebv} of $0.043$,
but $24\%$ of it exceeds $0.1$, $12\%$ exceeds $0.2$ and the largest is $1.06$, with a
fifth of the objects at Galactic latitude $|b| < 20^{\circ}$. No latitude or reddening cut
is imposed, so the approximation is worst for precisely the objects where it matters most.
